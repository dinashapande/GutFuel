How it works
Gut health & athletic performance

Your gut does more than digest food — it directly impacts how fast you run, how quickly you recover, and how sharp your focus is on game day.

⚡

Energy absorption

A healthy gut lining efficiently absorbs carbohydrates and fats for fuel. A damaged or inflamed gut means nutrients pass through without being used — leaving you running on empty even after a big meal.

💪

Muscle recovery

Gut bacteria produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) that reduce inflammation in muscle tissue. Better microbiome diversity = faster repair between training sessions.

🧠

Focus & mood

The gut-brain axis is a direct communication highway. Your microbiome regulates neurotransmitters like serotonin and dopamine — essential for focus, motivation, and mental toughness during competition.

🛡️

Immune defense

Athletes who train hard are more vulnerable to illness. 70% of immune function originates in the gut. A strong microbiome keeps you on the field instead of in bed.

💧

Hydration efficiency

Gut health affects how well your intestines absorb water and electrolytes. Poor gut function leads to dehydration and cramping even when you're drinking enough fluids.

😴

Sleep quality

Your microbiome influences melatonin production and sleep cycles. Deep sleep is when muscles grow — so a healthier gut means better sleep, and better sleep means bigger gains.

Nutrition guide
What to eat & what to skip

Feed the good bacteria. Starve the bad ones. Here's your cheat sheet for building a gut-friendly performance diet.

Eat more

Performance Foods

  • Fermented foods — yogurt, kefir, kimchi, sauerkraut (live cultures boost microbiome diversity)
  • High-fiber vegetables — broccoli, artichokes, garlic, onions (prebiotic fiber feeds good bacteria)
  • Whole grains — oats, quinoa, brown rice (slow-release energy + fiber)
  • Fatty fish — salmon, mackerel (omega-3s reduce gut inflammation)
  • Legumes — lentils, chickpeas, black beans (protein + prebiotic fiber combo)
  • Bananas — especially slightly unripe ones for resistant starch that feeds gut bacteria
  • Dark leafy greens — spinach, kale (magnesium for muscle + gut motility)
  • Bone broth — collagen and gelatin repair the gut lining
Limit these

Gut Disruptors

  • Processed sugar — feeds harmful bacteria and drives inflammation that slows recovery
  • Artificial sweeteners — aspartame and sucralose disrupt the microbiome balance
  • Fast food & fried foods — high in saturated fat, damages the intestinal lining
  • Alcohol — destroys beneficial bacteria and increases gut permeability ("leaky gut")
  • Highly processed snacks — chips, candy, packaged pastries offer zero microbiome benefit
  • Energy drinks — high sugar + caffeine combination stresses the gut and disrupts sleep
  • Antibiotic overuse — wipes out good bacteria; only use when medically necessary
  • NSAIDs before events — ibuprofen before competition irritates the gut lining

Game day
Game day nutrition guide

What you eat before, during, and after competition can make or break your performance. Here's the playbook — and what NOT to change on game day.

🍝

Carb loading (1–2 days before)

Increase carbohydrate intake in the 1–2 days before a big game to top off muscle glycogen stores. Aim for pasta, rice, potatoes, oats, and bread. This is your fuel tank — fill it up. Keep fat and fiber lower than usual so your gut has less to process on game day.

⏰

Pre-game meal (3–4 hrs before)

Eat a balanced meal with carbs, moderate protein, and low fat and fiber. Think: grilled chicken + white rice + a banana. Avoid anything high in fat, spice, or raw fiber — these slow digestion and can cause cramping mid-game. Drink 16–20 oz of water with this meal.

🍌

Pre-game snack (60–90 min before)

A small, easy-to-digest carb snack tops off energy and keeps blood sugar steady. Good options: banana, white toast with honey, sports chews, or a granola bar. Skip protein bars with high fat — they sit heavy. Target 30–60g of carbs for activities lasting 90+ minutes.

⚽

During activity (90+ min games)

For events or practices lasting longer than 90 minutes, aim for 30–60 grams of carbohydrates per hour. Sports drinks, gels, chews, or a banana at halftime all work. Pair with fluids to support absorption and prevent dehydration-related fatigue.

💪

Post-game recovery

Within 30–60 minutes after competition, prioritize carbs + protein + fluids together. The ratio: aim for roughly 3:1 carbs to protein (e.g. chocolate milk, a rice bowl with chicken, or a smoothie with fruit and Greek yogurt). This window is critical — muscle repair starts here.

🚫

Never try something new on game day

This is the golden rule of sports nutrition. Do not introduce a new food, drink, supplement, or eating pattern on game day. If your body hasn't practiced with it, your gut hasn't adapted to it. Stick entirely to what you've tested in training — even if a teammate swears by something.

Hydration
Hydration & athletic performance

Even mild dehydration — as little as 2% of body weight — can measurably hurt speed, strength, endurance, and decision-making. Here's how to stay ahead of it.

Daily baseline

How much to drink

  • General rule — half your body weight in ounces per day (e.g. 150 lb athlete = 75 oz minimum)
  • Before practice — drink 16–20 oz of water 2 hours before activity; another 8 oz 20 min before
  • During activity — 6–8 oz every 15–20 minutes; more in heat or humidity
  • After activity — for every pound of body weight lost during exercise, drink 16–24 oz to rehydrate
  • Urine check — pale yellow = well hydrated; dark yellow = drink more; clear = possibly overhydrated
Watch out for

Hydration mistakes

  • Waiting until you're thirsty — thirst is a late signal; by then you're already mildly dehydrated
  • Only drinking water for long sessions — activities over 60–90 min need electrolytes (sodium, potassium) not just water
  • Energy drinks as hydration — caffeine and sugar increase fluid loss and stress the gut
  • Skipping fluids with meals — digestion and nutrient absorption both require adequate hydration
  • Drinking too much at once — chugging large amounts right before competition can cause cramps and bloating
🧂

Electrolytes matter

Sweat doesn't just lose water — it loses sodium, potassium, magnesium, and chloride. These electrolytes control muscle contractions and nerve signals. Replacing only water without electrolytes during long sessions can lead to hyponatremia (dangerously low sodium) and muscle cramping.

🌡️

Heat & humidity

Athletes exercising in hot or humid conditions lose fluid significantly faster through sweat. In high heat, fluid needs can double. Pre-hydrate extra carefully before outdoor summer practices and games, and take every water break available — don't wait for a coach to offer one.

🧠

Brain performance

Dehydration hits your brain before your muscles. Even at 1–2% dehydration, reaction time slows, decision-making suffers, and focus drops. In sports where split-second choices matter, hydration is just as much a mental performance tool as it is a physical one.

Gut science
Prebiotics vs. probiotics

These two terms sound similar but play completely different roles. Both matter — and together, they're one of the most powerful tools for gut health.

Feed the bacteria

Prebiotics

What they are: Prebiotics are types of dietary fiber that your body can't digest — but your gut bacteria can. They act as food for the beneficial microbes already living in your gut, helping them grow and thrive.

Why athletes need them: More prebiotic fiber = more fuel for good bacteria = more short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) produced = less gut inflammation and faster recovery.

Best food sources:

  • Garlic & onions
  • Bananas (especially slightly unripe)
  • Oats & whole grains
  • Asparagus & artichokes
  • Legumes (lentils, chickpeas)
  • Leeks & chicory root
Add the bacteria

Probiotics

What they are: Probiotics are live bacteria and yeasts that you consume directly through food or supplements. When they reach your gut, they join your existing microbiome and help restore or diversify your bacterial community.

Why athletes need them: Heavy training suppresses immune function. Probiotics help maintain gut barrier integrity, reduce post-exercise inflammation, and support faster return to training after illness.

Best food sources:

  • Greek yogurt & kefir
  • Kimchi & sauerkraut
  • Kombucha (low sugar)
  • Miso & tempeh
  • Aged cheeses (gouda, cheddar)
  • Pickles (naturally fermented)
🔄

The synbiotic advantage

When you combine prebiotics and probiotics in the same meal, it's called a synbiotic. The live bacteria (probiotic) have an immediate food source (prebiotic) to survive and thrive on. Example: a bowl of oats (prebiotic) topped with kefir (probiotic) is more powerful for your gut than either alone. Build these combos into your daily meals for the biggest microbiome benefit.

Carb myths
Carb facts vs. myths

Carbohydrates are the most misunderstood macronutrient in sports nutrition. Let's clear up the biggest myths athletes believe.

Fact: Carbohydrates themselves don't cause fat gain — excess total calories do. For athletes, carbs are the primary fuel for high-intensity exercise. Your muscles store carbs as glycogen and burn it during activity. Undereating carbs as an athlete actually leads to muscle breakdown, fatigue, and hormonal disruption. The type and timing of carbs matters far more than the total amount.
Fact: Training fasted or low-carb forces your body to break down muscle protein for fuel — the opposite of what an athlete wants. Eating 30–60g of carbohydrates before a workout preserves muscle, sustains power output, and allows you to train harder and longer. While fat oxidation increases slightly when training fasted, total performance and muscle preservation both suffer significantly.
Fact: Carbohydrates vary enormously in how they affect your body. Complex carbs (oats, sweet potato, brown rice) digest slowly, provide sustained energy, and feed gut bacteria through their fiber content. Simple sugars (candy, soda, white bread) spike blood sugar rapidly and crash it just as fast. For gut health specifically, fiber-rich complex carbs are prebiotic — they directly nourish your microbiome. Simple sugars, especially in excess, feed harmful bacteria instead.
Fact: Protein cannot replace carbohydrates as a fuel source for high-intensity sports. The brain and working muscles during sprints, jumps, and explosive movements run almost exclusively on glucose (from carbs). Athletes on chronically low-carb diets often experience impaired performance, mood swings, poor sleep, hormonal disruption, and increased injury risk. Low-carb approaches may suit endurance athletes in very specific contexts, but for most young athletes in team sports, adequate carbohydrate intake is non-negotiable.
